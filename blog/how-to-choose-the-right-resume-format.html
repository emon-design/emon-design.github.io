How to Choose the Right Resume Format for Your Job Search

Chronological, skills-based, or something in between? A practical guide to choosing a resume format that fits your experience and the job you actually want.
By Deskwork · September 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Choosing a resume format sounds simple until you open a template collection and see ten different layouts staring back at you. One has a sidebar. Another puts skills at the top. A third looks almost like a traditional application form. Then there are templates that promise to be "perfect for any job," which is usually where things get confusing.

The truth is that the best resume format is not necessarily the most attractive one. It is the format that makes your particular background easy to understand and puts the information that matters most in the right place.

If you are a fresh graduate, your resume has a different story to tell than someone with ten years of experience. If you are changing careers, your recent job title may not tell the whole story either. The format should help explain that story rather than hide it.

Start With Your Career Situation, Not the Design

Before choosing colors, fonts, columns, or anything else, ask one question: What do I most need the person reading this resume to understand?

For an experienced applicant, that may be a clear record of recent work and achievements. For a student, it may be education, projects, internships, and practical skills. For a career changer, it may be the connection between previous experience and the new role.

That is why copying someone else's resume layout can be unhelpful. Their format was built around their experience, not yours.

Quick tipWrite down the three strongest things you want an employer to notice about you. Then choose a format that gives those things enough room instead of forcing everything into the same visual structure.

The Three Resume Formats You Will See Most Often

1. Reverse-chronological resume

This is the familiar format where your most recent experience appears first, followed by older positions. Education, skills, and other sections are usually placed around the work history.

For many applicants, this is the safest starting point. It gives the reader a straightforward picture of where you have worked and how your career has developed.

It works particularly well when your recent jobs are relevant to the position you are applying for and there are no large unexplained gaps that need extra context.

2. Functional or skills-based resume

A functional resume puts more attention on skills than on the timeline of employment. Someone with a complicated work history may be tempted to use this format because it can make job dates less prominent.

There is a catch: a reader may want to know where and when you actually gained those skills. Hiding the timeline does not necessarily make the underlying concern disappear.

For that reason, a completely skills-based resume is not always the best solution. A better approach for many people is to keep the employment history visible while giving relevant skills and achievements more attention.

3. Combination resume

A combination format brings together the two ideas. It can start with a short summary of relevant skills or strengths and then move into a clear work history.

This can be useful when your previous experience is valuable but your job titles do not immediately explain why you fit the new position.

For example, someone moving from customer support into sales might highlight communication, client relationship management, product knowledge, and performance results before showing the full employment history.

Which Format Is Best for a Fresh Graduate?

If you have recently graduated and do not have much full-time experience, you do not need to pretend that you have a ten-year career. Instead, make the experience you do have easier to see.

A clean student or entry-level format can give useful space to:

  • Education — degree, institution, dates, and relevant academic details.
  • Projects — coursework, university projects, personal projects, or practical work related to the position.
  • Internships — even short placements can demonstrate how you worked in a real organization.
  • Skills — technical and professional skills that actually relate to the job.
  • Certifications and training — especially when they support the role you want.
  • Relevant activities — leadership, volunteering, competitions, freelance work, or other evidence of responsibility.

Part-time work can also be worth including. A retail or restaurant job, for example, may not be directly related to an office position, but it can still show reliability, teamwork, communication, and responsibility if presented honestly.

What About Someone With Several Years of Experience?

For an experienced professional, the opposite problem often appears: there is too much information.

You may have fifteen years of work history, several qualifications, a long list of responsibilities, and enough projects to fill five pages. The solution is not to squeeze everything into a tiny font.

Start by deciding which experience is actually relevant to the job you are applying for. Recent positions usually deserve more attention than jobs from many years ago. Within each role, focus on achievements, important responsibilities, and work that demonstrates the skills the employer is looking for.

A structured chronological format usually works well here because it lets the reader follow your career without making them guess where each experience came from.

If You Are Changing Careers

Career changes are where resume format becomes more interesting.

Your current job title may have very little to do with the job you want next. That does not mean your previous experience is useless. You need to show the overlap.

Suppose you have worked in operations and now want a project coordination role. Your resume can bring attention to scheduling, vendor communication, documentation, process improvement, reporting, and coordinating people or deadlines. Those details create a bridge between the old role and the new one.

A combination-style format can work well because it lets you introduce those transferable skills before presenting the employment timeline.

Another useful testRead only your summary, skills, and most recent job. If those sections do not give a believable reason for the career change, changing the template will not solve the problem. The content needs work first.

Should You Use a Two-Column Resume?

Two-column resumes can look polished and make good use of space. A sidebar can hold contact information, skills, languages, or other supporting details while the main column carries the experience and education.

They are not automatically better, though. A two-column design becomes a problem when the text gets cramped or when the reading order is unclear.

Before choosing one, imagine the resume without its colors. Can you still tell where the main experience starts? Are dates easy to find? Are section headings obvious? Can the document be printed without becoming difficult to read?

If the answer is yes, the two-column structure may be perfectly reasonable. If the design only looks good because of decorative elements, choose something simpler.

What If the Job Posting Mentions ATS?

You may have heard that applicant tracking systems, or ATS software, require a very plain resume. The reality is more nuanced than that.

Readable text, clear section headings, standard job titles, and sensible formatting are good practices regardless of whether software is involved. Avoid turning important information into an image or relying on complicated visual tricks to communicate basic facts.

You also do not need to make every resume look like a plain text document. A clean professional design can still be readable and machine-friendly when the content and structure are sensible.

If an employer gives specific instructions about file type or formatting, follow those instructions first.

Do Not Choose a Format Just to Hide a Problem

This is one of the easiest mistakes to make.

Someone has a six-month employment gap, so they choose a format that hides dates. Someone has limited experience, so they add huge skill bars and graphics. Someone has changed jobs frequently, so they use a design where the employment history is difficult to follow.

A resume does not have to be perfect. It has to be understandable.

If there is a gap, explain it briefly when appropriate. If you are inexperienced, show projects and evidence of ability. If your career has moved around, make the relevant skills and results easy to see.

Trying to disguise information can make a reader work harder, and that usually is not what you want.

A Simple Way to Pick Your Format

If you are still unsure, this quick guide is a useful starting point:

  1. Recent experience is relevant and your career path is straightforward? Start with a reverse-chronological format.
  2. You are early in your career? Choose a clean student or entry-level format that gives education, projects, and skills enough room.
  3. You are changing careers? Consider a combination format that highlights transferable skills before the work history.
  4. Your work history is complicated? Keep the timeline understandable and use the content to explain your strengths rather than trying to hide dates.
  5. You work in a creative field? You can use more visual personality, but make sure the important information remains easy to read.

Formatting Matters More Than Decoration

Once you have chosen the basic structure, keep the design under control.

  • Use clear section headings.
  • Keep spacing consistent.
  • Make job titles and dates easy to locate.
  • Use a readable font size.
  • Keep bullet points reasonably short.
  • Do not use graphics simply because the template provides them.
  • Check how the finished resume looks as a PDF.

Most importantly, do not let the template dictate how much information you include. If a template gives you only three lines for your most important experience, it may simply be the wrong template for you.

Before You Send the Resume

Take a final pass with the job description beside you. You are not looking for an excuse to copy every phrase from the posting. You are checking whether the resume clearly demonstrates the qualifications that matter.

Then check the boring details. They are boring right up until you discover that a phone number has one wrong digit.

  • Is your name and contact information correct?
  • Are employment and education dates accurate?
  • Are job titles and company names consistent?
  • Are the most relevant skills easy to find?
  • Have you removed repeated or irrelevant information?
  • Does the resume look readable on both a phone and a larger screen?
  • Does the downloaded PDF still look the way you expected?
  • Did you follow the employer's instructions about file format or other requirements?

Frequently Asked Questions

Which resume format is best overall?

There is no single format that is best for every applicant. A reverse-chronological format is a strong starting point when your recent experience is relevant. Other formats can make more sense for students, career changers, or people with unusual work histories.

Should a fresh graduate use a functional resume?

Usually, it is better to show education, projects, internships, skills, and any relevant work clearly rather than hiding the lack of full-time experience behind a skills-only layout.

Is a two-column resume professional?

It can be. The important question is whether the information remains clear, readable, and easy to scan. A simple one-column resume can be just as professional.

Should I change my resume format for every job?

Not necessarily. Keep a strong base format and adjust the content for the position. Small changes to your summary, skills, or the order of relevant sections can be more useful than redesigning the entire resume for every application.

How long should a resume be?

There is no universal page count. Keep the document as concise as you can while preserving relevant evidence of your qualifications. Avoid shrinking the text just to force everything onto one page.

Can I use a resume template if I am applying in another country?

You can use a template as a starting point, but application expectations vary by country, employer, industry, and role. Check the specific requirements before submitting, especially for questions such as whether a photo or personal information is expected.

Final Thoughts

Your resume format should make your experience easier to understand. That is really the whole job.

Choose a chronological format when your career history tells a clear and relevant story. Give education, projects, and skills more room when you are starting out. If you are changing direction, use the format to make the connection between your past experience and your next role obvious.

And once you choose a template, remember that the template is only the frame. The useful part is still your actual experience, your work, and the evidence you can give for what you can do.
